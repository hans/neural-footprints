\begin{abstract}
The brain computes at vastly different levels of representation, encoding
both abstract knowledge and fine-grained sensory information about the
world. These two ends of the spectrum have distinct statistical properties:
while abstract knowledge about the world is compact and low-dimensional
(object identities, motion and collision, etc.), sensory information is
highly variable and difficult to compress (textures, patterns of color and
luminance). This statistical asymmetry creates fundamental issues for
neuroscience analysis techniques which rely on variance explained as a
measure of model fit (brain encoding, RSA, PCA). In a series of simulation
experiments, we demonstrate that variance-based techniques fail to
distinguish between obviously correct and incorrect models of visual
perception. While this leaves many neural analysis methods defunct, we show
that a successful model can be recovered if the analysis is required to
jointly model representation and computation. Reliable neural analysis therefore requires more than variance
explained: it demands models that link representations to the
computations and behaviors they support.
\end{abstract}

Visual perception requires the brain to maintain representations at
multiple levels of abstraction simultaneously. At one end, neurons encode
fine-grained sensory detail---textures, luminance gradients, color

of motion and collision that govern how a scene will unfold. These two
levels of representation have fundamentally different statistical

This statistical asymmetry poses a structural problem for the dominant
analysis methods in systems neuroscience. Encoding models, RSA, and PCA
all rely on variance explained as their measure of model fit
\citep{huth2016natural,yamins2014performance,kriegeskorte2008representational}.
When a high-dimensional sensory feature set and a low-dimensional
abstract feature set both contribute to neural activity, variance-based
methods will systematically privilege the sensory features at the expense of high level contents. The result
is that these methods cannot reliably distinguish between models that
capture the computationally relevant structure of a task and models that
merely overlap in their low level representations. 

We demonstrate this with a controlled simulation. Using PyBullet, we
construct partially-occluded physics scenes and generate synthetic neural
activity by random projection of the full program state (render buffers
$+$ serialized physics). This guarantees that both sensory and abstract
features are linearly present in the neural code. We then show that
encoding models, RSA, and PCA uniformly fail to detect the abstract
features. Critically, this failure is not a matter of statistical power:
it is a structural consequence of the statistical asymmetry between
representational levels. We then show that the correct model can be
recovered when the analysis is required to jointly account for
representation and computation---that is, to predict not just current
neural activity but where neural activity is headed. The implication is
that commonly used neural analysis methods are unreliable without
plausible cognitive models.

\section{Methods}
% ===================================================================

We simulate \nScenes{} scenes in PyBullet with random rigid objects
launched on a ground plane. A vertical wall at $x{=}0$ partially occludes
objects from the camera ($\imageSize{\times}\imageSize$ pixels,
\nTimesteps{} steps). The \emph{program state} concatenates all render
buffers (RGBA, depth, segmentation; 49{,}152 floats) with the serialized
physics engine state. \emph{Physics labels}---position, velocity, mass,
friction per object (75 floats)---are extracted separately via the API,
never concatenated into the program state, mirroring how neuroscientists
construct regressors.

Synthetic neural activity for \nNeurons{} units is:
$\mathbf{z} = \bar{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathbf{W}^\top + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$,
with $W_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\sqrt{D})$ and noise $\sigma = \noiseLevel$.
Since the program state contains both render and physics bytes, both are
linearly decodable in principle.

All analyses compare a \textbf{render model} (render bytes, PCA-reduced to
500 dims) against a \textbf{physics model} (75-dim API labels). Behavioral
sufficiency is measured by predicting future scene states from current
features via ridge regression.

\section{Results}
% ===================================================================

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/dissociation.pdf}
  \caption{Double dissociation between neural variance explained and
  behavioral sufficiency. Render features dominate encoding models but
  cannot predict future scene states; physics features predict behavior
  but are neurally invisible.}
  \label{fig:dissociation}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Double dissociation} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dissociation}).\quad
The render model explains most neural variance (cross-validated
$R^2 = \encodingRsqPixels$) but poorly predicts future scene states
($R^2 = \dynamicsRsqPixel$). The physics model accurately predicts future
dynamics ($R^2 = \dynamicsRsqPhysics$) but contributes almost nothing to
the encoding model ($\Delta R^2 = \encodingDeltaRsq$). The features that
explain the brain cannot explain the world, and vice versa.

\textbf{Encoding models.}\quad
A pixel-only model achieves $R^2 = \encodingRsqPixels$; adding all 75
physics variables yields $R^2 = \encodingRsqCombined$---negligible
$\Delta R^2 = \encodingDeltaRsq$. Standard encoding model logic would
conclude physics is not represented, yet we know by construction it is.
This false negative persists across neuron subsampling (10--500 units).
A control MLP predicting future dynamics from physics labels alone achieves
$\controlAccuracy\%$ accuracy, confirming the labels are informative.

\textbf{RSA.}\quad
Neural--render RDM correlation is $r = \rsaCorrNeuralRender$;
neural--physics is $r = \rsaCorrNeuralPhysics$ (\rsaRatio$\times$ ratio).
Partialling out render reduces neural--physics correlation to
$r = \rsaPartialNeuralPhysics$: the representational geometry is entirely
render-shaped.

\textbf{PCA.}\quad
The top PC explains \pcaVarExplainedOne\% of variance; 10 PCs explain
\pcaVarExplainedTen\%. These components are render-dominated. Decoding
object motion direction from leading PCs yields near-chance accuracy;
physics information resides in tail components only. Encoding models,
RSA, and PCA all miss the same causal variables for the same reason:
each privileges high-variance directions dominated by pixels.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{../figures/dynamics_analysis.pdf}
  \caption{Predicting future simulated brain states from initial-timestep
  features. Physics-based forward models far outperform render-based
  models, recovering the causal signal that static variance-based methods
  miss.}
  \label{fig:dynamics}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Dynamics} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamics}).\quad
The preceding analyses are static: they ask which features explain a
snapshot of neural activity. But if we instead ask which features predict
\emph{future} simulated brain states---neural activity at $t{+}n$ given
features at $t$---the picture inverts. A physics-based forward model
achieves $R^2 = \dynamicsRsqPhysics$; a render-based model achieves only
$R^2 = \dynamicsRsqPixel$---a gap of $\dynamicsGap$. Features that carry
the dynamics of the generative process, not its momentary appearance,
are what predict where the neural state is headed. This suggests a
concrete criterion for real data: models that predict brain states at
$t \gg n$ from features at $t$ should preferentially recover causally
operative variables.

\section{Discussion}
% ===================================================================

Our results do not challenge the correctness of encoding models, RSA, or
PCA---each faithfully identifies the dominant sources of variance in
neural activity. The problem is that variance explained is structurally
insufficient as a criterion for identifying the representations that
matter. The brain encodes both sensory detail and abstract knowledge, but
these occupy vastly different statistical regimes. Because abstract
knowledge is compact and low-dimensional, it is invisible to methods that
equate explanatory relevance with variance accounted for.

This statistical asymmetry is not an artifact of our simulation. It
reflects a general property of neural representation: the features that
dominate neural variance are not necessarily the features that the brain
computes over. In auditory cortex, spectrotemporal modulation features
explain substantial neural variance, but syntactic structure---which
determines sentence meaning---is low-dimensional and may be similarly
invisible to encoding models. In visual neuroscience, deep network
features dominate encoding performance throughout the ventral stream
\citep{yamins2014performance}, yet category-diagnostic or
affordance-relevant variables occupy a small subspace of those
representations. In motor cortex, high-dimensional kinematic features may
swamp the low-dimensional planning variables that govern action selection.
In each case, variance-based methods will find the statistically dominant
signal, not the computationally relevant one.

What distinguishes our positive result---the dynamics analysis---is that
it requires a model to jointly account for representation and computation.
Predicting future neural states from current features is not merely a
more demanding regression; it imposes a qualitatively different constraint.
A model that captures only the sensory surface of a scene cannot predict
where neural activity is headed, because the trajectory of neural
activity is governed by the causal structure of the world, not by its
momentary appearance. Only a model with access to the right level of
abstraction---the physical variables that determine how the scene
unfolds---can satisfy this criterion. The lesson generalizes: in real
neural data, candidate models should be evaluated not just by how much
variance they explain, but by whether they support predictions about
downstream neural states, motor outputs, or task-relevant decisions.
Variance explained tells you what dominates the signal. Cognitive insight
into what representations are \emph{for} is what tells you whether a
model is right.
